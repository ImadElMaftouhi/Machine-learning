# Clustering

Clustering is an unsupervised learning task where the goal is to partition a dataset into groups — clusters — such that instances within the same group are more similar to each other than to instances in other groups. Unlike classification, there are no labels. The algorithm must discover structure from the data alone.

This absence of labels makes clustering both more flexible and harder to evaluate. There is no single correct answer. Whether a clustering is "good" depends on the similarity measure chosen, the algorithm's assumptions about cluster shape, and the downstream use of the result.

# Why Clustering Matters

Clustering appears in practice as a standalone analysis tool (customer segmentation, anomaly detection, document grouping) and as a preprocessing step for supervised learning (using cluster assignments as features, or using clusters to guide data collection).

It also introduces the **Expectation-Maximization (EM)** algorithm, one of the most important iterative optimization frameworks in machine learning. Understanding EM through k-means and Gaussian mixture models builds intuition that carries forward into hidden Markov models, variational autoencoders, and other latent-variable models.

# Methods

## k-Means Clustering

k-Means partitions n points into k clusters by alternating between two steps: assigning each point to its nearest cluster centroid (expectation step), and recomputing each centroid as the mean of its assigned points (maximization step). This continues until assignments no longer change.

k-Means makes strong assumptions: clusters are spherical, roughly equal in size, and equal in density. When these assumptions hold, it is fast and effective. When they do not, it fails silently — it always produces k clusters regardless of whether the data has that structure.

Key practical issues: k must be chosen in advance (use the Elbow method or silhouette score), and the result depends on initialization (use k-means++ to mitigate this).

## Hierarchical Clustering (Agglomerative)

Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of nested clusters called a **dendrogram** by repeatedly merging the two closest clusters (agglomerative) or splitting the largest cluster (divisive). The result is not a single partition but a full hierarchy — the user chooses a cut point in the dendrogram to obtain a flat clustering.

The **linkage criterion** defines how distance between clusters is measured: single linkage (minimum pairwise distance), complete linkage (maximum pairwise distance), or Ward's method (minimize total within-cluster variance). Ward's method tends to produce compact, roughly equal clusters and is the most common choice.

Hierarchical clustering does not require specifying k in advance and produces a richer output than k-means, but it is more expensive: O(n² log n) for efficient implementations.

## Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

A Gaussian Mixture Model assumes that the data was generated by a mixture of k Gaussian distributions, each with its own mean and covariance. Training fits the parameters of these Gaussians using the **full EM algorithm**: the E-step computes soft assignments (the probability that each point belongs to each Gaussian), and the M-step updates the Gaussian parameters to maximize the expected log-likelihood.

GMMs generalize k-means in two ways: assignments are soft (probabilistic) rather than hard, and clusters can take elliptical shapes (not just spherical) because each Gaussian has its own covariance matrix. The tradeoff is higher computational cost and more parameters to estimate.

GMMs are the first fully probabilistic model in this module. Understanding the EM derivation here is direct preparation for more complex latent-variable models.

# Evaluation

Clustering has no ground-truth labels, so evaluation is more subtle than in supervised learning.

- **Inertia (within-cluster sum of squares)**: used by k-means. Lower is better, but always decreases with more clusters — use the Elbow method to find the point of diminishing returns.
- **Silhouette score**: measures how similar a point is to its own cluster compared to the nearest other cluster. Ranges from -1 to 1; higher is better. Works across algorithms.
- **Dendrogram**: for hierarchical clustering, visualize the merge order and distances to choose the number of clusters.
- **Log-likelihood**: for GMMs, monitor convergence of the EM algorithm. A plateauing log-likelihood means convergence.

When ground-truth labels are available (e.g., on a benchmark dataset), you can also use **Adjusted Rand Index** or **Normalized Mutual Information** to compare the discovered clusters to the true labels.

# What to Observe

- Run k-means with random initialization multiple times on the same data. Observe that different initializations produce different results. Then switch to k-means++ and compare stability.
- Compare k-means and GMM on a dataset with elongated or anisotropic clusters. k-Means will fail; GMM with full covariance will succeed.
- Plot the GMM log-likelihood across EM iterations. It should increase monotonically and plateau — this is the EM convergence guarantee.
- Use agglomerative clustering with different linkage criteria on the same data. Note how single linkage tends to produce chain-like clusters while Ward produces compact ones.
